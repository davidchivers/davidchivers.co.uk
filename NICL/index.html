Research

How people lead and follow

Someone takes charge in a meeting. Their colleagues may welcome the initiative, ignore it or turn to someone else. Network researchers study these exchanges by looking at how people see themselves, how groups respond and what people remember afterwards.

Identity

When do people see themselves as leaders?

In one study, 69 young adults at a UK university recorded how strongly they identified as leaders and as followers over three separate weeks. On days when they experienced an unusual or disruptive event, they tended to see themselves more strongly as leaders. The researchers found a similar pattern in familiar and unfamiliar settings.

Read the study on identity ↗link.springer.com

Simulation

How does a group come to share leadership?

A computer simulation lets researchers follow a team one exchange at a time. One member tries to lead and another decides whether to follow. By varying how closely each person fits the others’ idea of a leader, researchers can explore when influence spreads across the group and when it concentrates in one person.

Explore the simulation study ↗sciencedirect.com

Memory and ratings

What are we measuring when we rate a leader?

Think of a manager you trust. Can you recall a particular thing they did, or are you drawing on a general impression? Across six studies, researchers tested how survey questions prompt each kind of memory. Asking people which kind they used reduced the influence of personal liking on their ratings.

Read the study on memory and ratings ↗sciencedirect.com

Memory and events

What does memory have to do with seeing yourself as a leader?

In an experiment, students learned about energy use alone or with a partner, then made a plan or watched someone else do it. Among those who learned with a partner and made their own plan, students who were better at linking details in memory felt more like leaders and more confident about leading.

Predicting leadership self-concepts ↗link.springer.com

Leadership development

How do the other roles in your life shape the way you lead?

A team leader may also be a colleague, a parent or a carer. The authors propose a model for exploring how these identities fit together and where they pull in different directions. They offer exercises and reflection questions that leaders and educators can use to explore those connections.

Leadership Starts from Within ↗journals.aom.org
