The effects of what you remember and what you know on leadership processes: How memory works, how we access what we know, and the role of identity levels

Summary

Memory plays a key role in our ability to accurately rate leaders, and for our ability to access our own leadership skills. Measures of leadership that emphasize processes associated with episodic memory (of separate, contextualized events) rather than semantic memory processes (abstractions from multiple events) can be especially useful in understanding and developing leadership. The nature of our leader identities, which can focus on the individual, relational or collective level, influences our likelihood of engaging in leadership behaviors.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of the chapter, readers should:

1. Understand how memory operates and the key distinction between semantic and episodic memory processes.

2. Be aware of their highest LSCS-R identity and motivational orientation scores and the potential effects of these on their own perceptions of others, and use of social and task skills.

3. Be able to describe their own leader identity level and how it influences their own behavior and how others view them.

4. Be able to identity whether and how their own leadership behaviors affect the identity levels of others.

Personal Development Activities

Working alone or in small groups, describe some specific interactions that you have had which involve leadership (in these interactions, you might have taken the role of the leader, a follower, or simply an observer). Consider what level of identity (individual, relational, collective) the key persons in these interactions likely held. Were those persons acting from similar or different identities? How might the interaction outcomes have been different if the identity levels differed? You might want to try role playing the same interaction multiple times whilst adopting different identity levels.

Self-reflection on your own typical identity levels.

As you consider the information just presented, it may be helpful to look back at the self-ratings that you recorded in the “My Score” column of Table 2 and address the following questions.

Which level of identity (that is, the Individual Identity, Relational Identity or Collective Identity subscale score) received the highest value? Do you think that this identity level has shaped the way you see others, the skills that you have developed and normally use, or the way that you feel about things?

How has it affected my:

Perceptions of others?

Use of skills?

Feelings?

Then look back at your motivational orientation scores in Table 2 (that is, the subscales of Comparative Identity, Concern for Others, Group Achievement). Which orientation received the highest score? Was the highest rated motivational orientation consistent with your expectations? What do you believe are the strengths and weaknesses of this motivational orientation for your current leadership situation?

How has it influenced aspects of my motivation:

Task effects?

Social effects?

Self-reflection on your use of identities for leadership.

To see the personal relevance of these points, think back on some of your most recent leadership experiences. You might find it helpful to consider some or all of the following questions.

Which identities were active for you?

What type of identities did you prime in others?

Did you typically create an environment that emphasizes learning, or did it emphasize performance and avoiding errors?

How do you think your leadership identity level has affected your leadership behavior and the way you are perceived by others?

How has it affected your motivation as a leader?
