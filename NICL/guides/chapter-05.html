Summary

This chapter explains how leader self-complexity can enhance adaptability and how high-quality, diverse career experiences can foster this self-complexity. To support your development, the chapter presents two self-assessment tools that allow you to evaluate your level of leader self-complexity and your career experiences, helping you better understand your unique characteristics. The insights and strategies outlined in this chapter are instrumental not only for individual leadership development but also for those seeking to promote leadership growth within their organizations.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of the chapter, readers should:

1. Understand what leader self-complexity is and why it is important for adaptability.

2. Be aware that leader self-complexity can be developed through high-quality, diverse career experiences.

3. Be able to use the two self-assessment tools provided in this chapter to evaluate your leader self-complexity and career experiences.

4. Be able to use the two self-assessment tools provided in this chapter to design your own leadership development plan.

Case Questions

1. Based on the case study of Jane and Sarah, explain why Jane’s leader self-concept is considered complex, whereas Sarah’s leader self-concept is considered simple. What are the key differences in their leader self-concepts?

2. If you were in Sarah's position, what steps would you take to develop your leader self-complexity? Outline a plan that includes specific strategies and experiences to help you broaden and deepen your leader self-concept.

3. Identify and discuss the evidence presented in the chapter that supports the idea that Jane is a more adaptive leader compared to Sarah. How does Jane’s complex leader self-concept contribute to her adaptability?

Personal Development Activities

In this chapter, we have provided two self-assessment tools (see Tables 5.1, 5.2, and 5.3) to help you evaluate your current leader self-complexity and career experiences. These tools are not only for assessment but can also serve as powerful instruments for your personal development. After reading this chapter, you may wish to further develop your leader self-complexity through intentional career experiences. The activity outlined below is designed to guide you in this process.

Review Leadership Roles: Start by revisiting the list of leadership roles in Table 5.1. Identify any aspects of your leader self-concept that you would like to add or further develop in the future. As you do this, consider which aspects are achievable in the short term (within one year or less) and which may require a longer-term focus.

Set Development Goals: Once you have identified the leadership roles you wish to develop, write down the role labels at the top of a piece of paper. Under each role, list the corresponding characteristics (such as traits, skills, and self-regulation strategies) that you aim to cultivate. You can use Table 5.2 as a starting point for this step. Additionally, think about the types of experiences that could help you achieve these developmental goals. The questions on "diversity of work experience" in Table 5.3 can provide further guidance.

Example: If you want to incorporate the role of "team builder" into your leader self-concept, begin by listing this role at the top of your page. Underneath, you might identify traits like "effective communication," "empathy," and "cultural competence." Next, consider experiences that could facilitate the development of these traits. For instance, in the short term, you might set a goal to have one meaningful conversation with a team member each week. In the mid-term, you could aim to work with different departments within your organization. For a long-term goal, you might seek opportunities to work in diverse cultural settings or industries.

Evaluate Development Opportunities: As you progress in your career, you will encounter various opportunities that could help you achieve your developmental goals. Before committing to these opportunities, use the questions on "quality of work experience" in Table 5.3 to evaluate them critically. For example, before relocating to another department or industry, consider whether the new experience will offer you autonomy, provide clear feedback, and ensure adequate support.

Create a Personal Action Plan: Finally, organize the goals and strategies you've identified under each leadership role label. Use this as your personal action guide on your journey to becoming a more effective leader.

By following these steps, you will be well-equipped to systematically develop your leader self-complexity, aligning your career experiences with your personal growth objectives.

Table 5.1 Roles potentially held by leaders

bMentor

Facilitates the development and growth of the employees.

bInnovator

Is creative and envisions, encourages, and facilitates change.

bMonitor

Checks on the progress and quality of the work and evaluates the performance of members.

aNegotiator

Negotiates agreements and coordinates related activities with other parts of the organization or with outsiders.

cFacilitator

Delegates responsibility and authority to employees and involves employees in making important work-related decisions.

aSpokesperson

Transmits information and expresses value statements to people outside their organizational subunit.

aResource

allocator

Exercises their authority to allocate resources such as money, personnel, material, equipment, facilities, and services.

cCompetitor

Focuses on the competition by emphasizing the need to compete, developing a competitive focus, and insisting on beating outside competitors.

eTeam builder

Fosters teamwork, develops a positive team atmosphere, facilitates collective learning, and builds team cohesion.

cMotivator

Motivates members by praising, providing recognition, and recommending appropriate rewards, and inspires people to exceed expectations

dPlanner

Develops short-term plans for the work and determines the action steps and resources needed to accomplish a project or activity.

cProducer

Shows a hard work ethic by showing an appetite for hard work, demonstrating full exertion on the job, and modeling an intense work effort.

fCommunicator

Relates messages between subordinates and top management, interprets and communicates the strategies set out by top management, and provides relevant information to the executive committee to facilitate strategy formulation.

bBroker

Acquires resources and maintains the unit’s external legitimacy through the development, scanning, and maintenance of a network of external contacts.

bCoordinator

Provides tight project management, maintains the structure, does the scheduling, coordinating, and problem-solving, and sees that rules and standards are met.

bDirector

Engages in goal setting and role clarification, sets priorities and deadlines for important aspects of the work, and establishes clear expectations for individuals.

Note: aadapted from Mintzberg (1975), badapted from Denison et al. (1995), cadapted from Lawrence et al. (2009), dadapted from Yukl (2012), eadapted from Cameron et al. (2014), adapted from f Rensburg et al. (2014).
