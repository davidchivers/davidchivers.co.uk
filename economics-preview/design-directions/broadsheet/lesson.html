What £24 buys

Two goods, one budget, and a line that moves when life changes. Read on and the figure follows you; then try it yourself.

An illustrative explainer drawing on the budget-set lesson in Introduction to Economics. The original course is unchanged.

1 The line

You have £24. Coffee costs £3 and lunch £6. Spend it all on coffee and you get 8; spend it all on lunch and you get 4.

The straight line between those two points is every basket that spends exactly £24. Everything underneath it is affordable too: together they make the budget set.1

2 On the line

Four coffees and two lunches: £12 + £12 = £24. A basket on the line leaves nothing over.

3 Inside

Two coffees and two lunches cost £18. That is affordable with £6 to spare, so the point sits inside the shaded set.

4 Outside

Six coffees and three lunches would cost £36: £12 more than you have. Anything beyond the line is out of reach.

5 The slope is a price

Slide along the line and every extra coffee costs you half a lunch: £3 ÷ £6. That ratio is the opportunity cost of a coffee, and it is the slope of the line, −0.5.

6 More income

Income rises to £36; prices don’t change. Both ends move out, to 12 coffees and 6 lunches, and the slope stays −0.5. The line shifts out in parallel.

The old basket still costs £24, leaving £12 unspent.

7 Dearer coffee

Back to £24, but coffee now costs £6. Lunch hasn’t changed, so you can still buy 4 lunches: that end stays put. The coffee end falls from 8 to 4.

The line pivots inwards and steepens to −1. The old basket would now cost £36: £12 over.

8 What the line can’t tell you

Every point on the line spends exactly £24. Which one is best? The budget set can’t say. Affordability alone never picks an optimum; for that we need preferences, which is where utility maximisation comes in.

Practise

Exercises

All three use the opening example, £24, coffee £3, lunch £6, whatever the sandbox shows. Answers are checked on this page; nothing is recorded.

1 You buy two coffees. What is the most lunch you can afford?

Show the answer

Three lunches. Two coffees cost £6, leaving £18: exactly three £6 lunches. Four lunches would bring the total to £30.

2 Income rises to £36 and both prices stay the same. What happens to the budget line?

Show the answer

It shifts out in parallel. Both intercepts rise, to 12 coffees and 6 lunches; with prices unchanged the slope stays −0.5.

3 Back to £24, but coffee rises to £6. Which statement is right?

Show the answer

The most lunch is still 4, because lunch still costs £6; the most coffee falls from 8 to 4. The line pivots inwards and its slope becomes −1.
