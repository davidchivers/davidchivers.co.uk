# Making slides with AI: example notes

These are invented teaching examples, not empirical findings. Use them to practise
making time-series and distribution charts, a worked calculation and a revealable
answer. Use your own visual design; the example uses cream, teal and orange.

## 1. Compare spending over time

Use invented monthly household spending, in pounds, for one illustrative household:

| Year | Food (£/month) | Energy (£/month) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 2020 | 240 | 80 |
| 2021 | 252 | 84 |
| 2022 | 264 | 96 |
| 2023 | 288 | 112 |
| 2024 | 300 | 120 |

Plot both series as lines with markers, direct labels and distinct line styles.
Let the reader switch between pounds and percentage growth since 2020.
Percentage growth = 100 * (current value / 2020 value - 1).

- Food growth: 0%, 5%, 10%, 20%, 25%.
- Energy growth: 0%, 5%, 20%, 40%, 50%.
- Food has the larger cash increase: £300 - £240 = £60, versus £120 - £80 = £40.
- Energy has the larger percentage increase: 50%, versus 25%.

Label the units and update the vertical scale when switching views: £0–£400 in
pounds, 0–60% for growth. Both growth series start at zero percent. Keep the years
and data unchanged. Show the complete pounds view initially. An instant switch
is enough for the first version. If asked to add animation later, use a short,
user-triggered transition with an instant alternative for reduced motion.
These figures are invented spending, not inflation estimates or observed data.

## 2. Reveal what the average hides

Use two groups of ten invented scores, measured out of 100:

- Group A: 40, 42, 44, 46, 48, 52, 54, 56, 58, 60.
- Group B: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 75, 80, 85, 90, 95.

Both sum to 500 and average 50. Group A ranges from 40 to 60; B from 5 to 95.
These are synthetic examples, not student records. Give each group one row of dots
on the same 0–100 axis. Let a button collapse the dots to the mean, then reveal the
individual scores. Keep the axes fixed; start with the complete distributions.

## 3. Show the calculation

Treat the ten scores as the complete population of each illustrative group.
Mean mu = 500/10 = 50. Define x_i as score i and sigma as population standard deviation.
Population variance is sigma^2 = (1/10)*sum((x_i - 50)^2).

- Group A: sum of squared deviations=440; variance=44; standard deviation=sqrt(44)=6.63324958.
- Group B: sum of squared deviations=12750; variance=1275; standard deviation=sqrt(1275)=35.70714214.

Round the standard deviations to 6.63 and 35.71 score points. The mean is the same,
but the spread differs. If estimating variance from a sample instead, the usual
unbiased estimator divides by n-1; that is not the quantity illustrated here.

## 4. Predict before revealing

Start with 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. Replace 6 with 46.
Before: total=20, mean=4, median=4.
After: total=60, mean=12, median=4.
The mean responds to the size of the extreme value; the median is still the middle
ordered value. These are invented data for a small revealable question.

## Checks

Check the spending values, percentage-growth calculations and labelled axes;
both score means and variances; the mean/median calculation; keyboard controls;
and transitions that stop when leaving a slide. Keep useful static and
reduced-motion views. The prepared HTML is an example, not proof that a student's
assistant generated it correctly. Inspect the output and explain any changes.
