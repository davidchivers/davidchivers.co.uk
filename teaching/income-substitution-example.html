Model, assumptions and calculations

This is a two-good Cobb–Douglas illustration, with positive quantities, a fixed £12 income and a fixed £1 price for Y. It uses Hicksian compensation: B faces the new prices but reaches exactly the original utility. B is a hypothetical comparison; actual income stays £12.

The initial choice is A = (3, 6), with utility √18. Writing p for the new price of X, the compensated choice is B = (√(18/p), √(18p)), requiring income 2√(18p). The final choice is C = (6/p, 6).

Substitution in X is xB − xA; income is xC − xB. They add to the total, xC − xA. Calculations use full precision; displayed values are rounded.

Why does Y finish at 6? These preferences allocate half the budget to each good. With income and the price of Y fixed, the final quantity of Y stays at 6. Its substitution and income effects offset. That is a feature of this example, not a general prediction for all preferences. Both goods here are normal; the model does not show inferior or Giffen goods.

The stages separate two economic effects, not a claim that people literally make these choices in sequence. Slutsky compensation uses a different finite-change comparison: making the original bundle affordable. Follow the convention in your course.
