Question and closest predecessors

Urban renewal and highways are different treatments. Renewal can destroy destinations and displace their users; a highway can sever nearby trips while improving longer journeys. Pooling them as historical infrastructure obscures the mechanism. The useful common question is whether the inherited urban layout constrains which populations can reach the same destinations, beyond its association with where people live. That requires distinguishing residential sorting, destination supply, travel impedance and actual visitor composition.

H1. Collins and Shester, AEJ: Applied Economics 2013, “Slum Clearance and Urban Renewal in the United States.” Publication metadata and abstract were verified. The inspected October 2010 working-paper strategy instruments city renewal grants per 1950 resident with the period enabled by state legislation, examining 1980 outcomes conditional on 1950 characteristics. Final-paper text was unavailable, so exact final specification equivalence remains unchecked. City-level gains cannot establish benefits for displaced residents or businesses; the early manuscript explicitly distinguishes those questions. Publisher; inspected earlier paper.

H2. Baum-Snow, QJE 2007, “Did Highways Cause Suburbanization?” Publisher metadata and abstract verify a 1950-1990 central-city population design using planned interstate routes as instruments for constructed highways. It addresses suburbanization, not encounters or destination-level racial mixing. The full final methods were not retrieved here; the metropolitan-level result cannot validate a parcel-scale instrument merely by citation. Publisher.

H3. Aiello, Vybornova, Juhasz, Szell and Bokanyi, PNAS 2025, “Urban highways are barriers to social ties.” Published metadata and the March 2025 author preprint were inspected. Mutual Twitter-follow ties, inferred homes and highway geography in 50 metropolitan areas are compared with rewired networks preserving degree, distance and density features. The authors explicitly describe their evidence as correlational. A network null model is not randomized highway placement; online ties are not observed face-to-face contact. Published DOI; inspected author text.

H4. Brinkman and Lin, Review of Economics and Statistics 2024, “Freeway Revolts! The Quality of Life Effects of Highways.” Final metadata were verified through the author’s publication page. The July 2022 manuscript inspects neighborhood population patterns, planned/historical-route instruments, Detroit travel evidence and a spatial model of amenities and access. Its instruments were redigitized because metropolitan route measures lacked neighborhood detail. Detailed final-version equivalence was not checked. This is a close predecessor for access-versus-barrier mechanisms, not merely generic highway background. Author publication record; inspected manuscript.

H5. Mahajan, JUE 2024, “Highways and segregation.” Publisher-indexed abstract and text describe census-tract racial sorting and metropolitan segregation, instrumenting highways with the 1947 plan, shortest-route variants and historical exploration routes. Full final methods were not independently audited. The volume year is 2024; the DOI and online appearance are 2023. Residential racial sorting is therefore already a direct outcome in this literature. Publisher DOI.

H6. Brinkman and Lin, “The Costs of Access: Freeway Disamenities Across US Cities,” August 2026 manuscript. The author page lists acceptance at Regional Science and Urban Economics, but no final DOI was verified. Selected model and discussion sections construct city disamenity measures by removing estimated amenity costs while holding modeled travel times fixed, then relate these measures to growth. This is model-dependent counterfactual separation and cross-city association, not independent experimental identification of social barriers. Current manuscript; status record.

The specific Green Book renewal predecessor is G2, digested once in idea 5. Its boundary comparison makes a proposal to estimate renewal effects on listed businesses particularly close to existing work. A new modern outcome is not by itself a new identification strategy.

Remaining contribution

A potentially nontrivial contribution would measure how the same historical barrier separates destination choices of nearby origins, holding the distinction between origins and destinations explicit. This differs conceptually from residential segregation, aggregate city decline and online friendship. For example, residents of adjacent areas may have comparable menus within a short Euclidean distance but very different travel-time access to shared destinations. The proposed contribution is to measure that constraint and its demographic incidence, not to rediscover that cities are divided.

For renewal, the analogous question concerns the loss and replacement of a destination network. How much opportunity for shared destination use is absent because commercial sites disappeared, and how much reflects sorting among sites that remain? That is a site-supply accounting exercise unless credible historical variation identifies the counterfactual. The original residents’ welfare is a third question and cannot be answered by following modern parcels alone.

These distinctions could yield an informative descriptive paper even without a causal effect of infrastructure. A causal paper would need a specific project, documented timing, suitable pretreatment observations and a defensible comparison. The historical label is not an identification device. Nor should a successful infrastructure first stage be repurposed as an instrument for mixing in an attitudes equation: construction affects jobs, property values, pollution, travel and residential selection through many other paths.

Data and unit requirements

The available project record establishes modern annual measurement work, not a ready historical infrastructure panel. It does not establish verified renewal polygons, project dates, route vintages, data permissions or a link to modern destinations. The existing geographical and upstream measurement questions still apply. This review made no data-access attempt and does not infer usability from public maps or replication links.

A corridor study would require the distinction between proposed and built alignments, opening dates, actual crossings, access ramps, pedestrian connectivity and relevant travel times. A renewal study would require parcel or project boundaries, demolition versus approval dates, phased construction, preexisting land use and displaced destinations. Coarse annual program totals cannot supply those details. A historical project name can survive boundary changes and multiple phases; treating its first recorded date as a single intervention risks severe timing error.

Choose the analysis unit before linkage. An origin-pair design concerns whether populations share destinations. A parcel design concerns local survival and replacement. A resident-cohort design concerns displacement and later outcomes. A city-level design concerns aggregate adjustment. Each requires a different denominator and counterfactual. Reporting a parcel result as if it describes the original population is an ecological and selection error, not simply imperfect wording.

Design and identification threats

A feasible descriptive starting point would compare observed shared-destination use for origin pairs across versus along a barrier, using predeclared distance bands and a common destination universe. Account for geographic separation, crossings, trip purpose and feasible travel time rather than treating straight-line distance as access. However, choosing a distance threshold after seeing the contrast would overstate the evidence. Rivers, railways, zoning and earlier social boundaries can coincide with highways and must remain plausible alternative explanations.

The same care applies to spatial discontinuities. Close locations on opposite sides of a highway are not necessarily comparable before construction. Housing markets and population groups may have sorted along a preexisting divide, and the alignment itself may have been chosen to exploit low land values or weak political opposition. Current covariate balance cannot establish historical exchangeability. Conditioning on current income or race may also remove part of the treatment pathway while leaving earlier selection uncontrolled.

Modern 2017-2019 outcomes cannot provide a before-and-after event study of construction decades earlier. Adding year effects does not repair the missing pretreatment period. A historical cross-section can estimate a conditional association, or support a separately defended instrumental-variable design, but not validate an event-study pretrend that was never observed. Conversely, recent removal or reconnection projects create different treatments, often bundled with redevelopment and changing land uses; their effects need not reverse original construction effects.

Spillovers are central rather than optional robustness checks. A new crossing may redirect trips from neighboring crossings, and a demolished commercial district may be replaced elsewhere. Spatial buffers treated as independent controls can therefore be affected by the same project. Define whether the estimand is local redistribution or metropolitan net change, and cluster or otherwise model uncertainty at the effective project or corridor level. The number of destinations is not the number of independent interventions.

Outcome-blind feasibility and recommendation

Require one written design dossier before any approved empirical pilot: treatment definition, historical chronology, comparison construction, data rights, expected spatial precision and the outcome window. Examine support and linkage quality without viewing mixing contrasts. A design with no usable pretreatment period must explicitly adopt a descriptive or separately justified long-run framework. Do not revive a failed contemporary support design simply by substituting an older event name.

Proceed only if the dossier resolves the unit, denominator and counterfactual and explains the incremental contribution over H3-H6 and G2. Otherwise retain the idea as a reserve source of mechanisms for the core descriptive paper. Its ambition is substantial, but its linkage and identification burden is higher than a bounded historical-site feasibility audit.
