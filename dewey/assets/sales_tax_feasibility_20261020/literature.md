# Sales taxes, destination choice and income mixing

Verified: 20 October 2026. Bounded public-primary-source review, seven lead
families. Question: do local sales-tax changes alter income-specific destination
choices and market-wide visitor-origin mixing, rather than only spending or
traffic? This is a literature overlap note, not an econometric design or event
inventory. Repository/project AGENTS, RESEARCH_STYLE, STATUS, README, memory and
DATA_POLICY were checked. No private/raw data, Teams, messages, external-model
submissions, empirical runs or shared-record updates were used.

## Assessment

**Keep the proposal as a research question, not a verified novelty claim.**
Tax-induced spatial shopping substitution, income heterogeneity and
mobility-based income composition each have direct precedents below. Their
intersection must be checked, especially against Miller and Omartian. A decline
in total visits does not establish changed visitor shares; changed shares at
one destination do not establish increased mixing across the market. These are
logical distinctions, not results from this review.

The closest named sales-tax/mobility paper is still **listing-only**. Among the
retrieved evidence, no study was verified to estimate the complete chain from
local sales-tax changes through income-specific destination reallocation to
market-wide mixing. That is an access/search result, not evidence that such a
paper does not exist.

## Verified leads

### 1. Gregory S. Miller and James D. Omartian

**Consumer Response to Sales Tax Changes: Evidence from Mobility Data.**
**Access: listing only; manuscript missing from this review.** Oxford's
[2021 programme](https://oxfordtax.sbs.ox.ac.uk/files/cbtacademicsymposium2021pdf),
PDF p. 2, schedules Omartian with Miller on **6 July 2021**, 16:30-17:15;
Ben Lockwood is the discussant. The
[event page](https://oxfordtax.sbs.ox.ac.uk/event/academic-symposium-2021)
confirms that listing. [Miller's CV](https://www.bus.umich.edu/facultybios/cv/millerg.pdf),
PDF p. 4, calls it a **2022 working paper** under papers in process.
[Omartian's CV](https://www.bus.umich.edu/FacultyBios/CV/omartian.pdf),
PDF p. 1, updated **13 November 2025**, also lists it as a working paper.
These are different record/version dates, not a verified publication date.

Exact-title, author-pair, PDF and SSRN-oriented searches, plus Oxford and the
institutional CVs, did not yield an accessible manuscript or author abstract.
The title establishes direct topic overlap only. Income groups, mobility
provider, destination definitions, clientele composition, mixing outcomes,
identification and estimates all remain **unverified**. Do not describe it as
"traffic only" or repeat numerical estimates attributed to it by secondary
sources. No author was contacted.

### 2. Scott R. Baker, Stephanie Johnson and Lorenz Kueng (2021)

**Shopping for Lower Sales Tax Rates**, *AEJ: Macroeconomics* 13(3), 209-250,
**July 2021**, DOI 10.1257/mac.20190026.
**Access: published abstract and full published supplement; full November 2017
author draft. Final main-text PDF not retrieved.** The
[published abstract](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/mac.20190026)
establishes state/local tax changes, stockpiling, online and cross-border
substitution, and taxable/exempt spending complementarity.

The [author-hosted draft](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.northwestern.edu/dist/a/1657/files/2017/02/SalesTax_BJK-272aozo.pdf),
PDF pp. 27 and 42, measures household spending outside the home ZIP3/state.
Table 6's average short-run alternative-ZIP response is insignificant; not
every cross-border specification is significant. The
[published supplement](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles/materials/14974),
PDF p. 18, Table A.3, explicitly tests income heterogeneity: the income
interaction is 0.047 (SE 1.349). High/low-income subsamples are also reported;
different subgroup significance is not a test of their difference.

**Overlap:** spatial allocation and income heterogeneity are already examined,
but the verified income test concerns spending, not income-specific destination
shares. Venue clientele and market-wide mixing are not established by these
materials. Do not equate ZIP3 purchase locations with venue-level co-presence.

### 3. Sumit Agarwal, Nathan Marwell and Leslie McGranahan (2017)

**Consumption Responses to Temporary Tax Incentives: Evidence from State Sales
Tax Holidays**, *AEJ: Economic Policy* 9(4), 1-27, **November 2017**,
DOI 10.1257/pol.20130429. **Access: final abstract; full May 2013 author
manuscript. Final article PDF requires access and was not retrieved.** The
[publisher abstract](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pol.20130429)
reports spending increases on eligible goods without offsetting reductions
before/after the holidays in the studied windows.

The [author's download page](https://www.ushakrisna.com/publications/consumption-response-to-temporary-tax-incentives-evidence-from-state-sales-tax-holidays)
links this [May 2013 manuscript](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/699fd8b75d25b73272aa329d/699fd8b75d25b73272aa3415_ssrn-2178753%20%281%29.pdf).
Section 4.2.2, PDF pp. 17-18, examines income groups in both diary and card
samples and reports **no consistent income ranking across samples**. Section
4.5, PDF pp. 24-25, examines border spillovers, but explicitly lacks merchant
locations: increased spending by nearby out-of-state residents does not confirm
where they shopped.

**Overlap:** income-specific spending and geographic spillovers, not directly
observed destination switching, venue clientele or mixing. These detailed
findings are verified for the 2013 version; their retention in the final 2017
article remains unverified. Temporary, product-specific holidays are not
automatically equivalent to persistent general local-tax changes.

### 4. Agarwal, Souphala Chomsisengphet, Wenlan Qian and Weibiao Xu

**Tax Differential and Cross-Border Shopping: Evidence from Singapore.**
**Access: author-deposited abstract only.** The
[SSRN record](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3038550)
is dated **17 September 2017**, revised **12 April 2018**; do not label it a
verified 2018 journal publication. Its download route returned the abstract
page, not a readable manuscript.

The abstract reports lower domestic transaction spending near the border when
domestic sales tax is higher, particularly for substitutable purchases; it
reports no corresponding difference for utilities/services or direct
marketing. It also examines domestic store sales near the border.

**Overlap:** geographic substitution and store demand, beyond undifferentiated
aggregate spending. **Unverified:** income-specific responses, direct foreign
destination observations, clientele shares, mixing and the precise tax-change
identification. Domestic spending declines alone do not establish the mix of
people attending particular foreign stores.

### 5. Lesley Chiou and Erich Muehlegger (2008)

**Crossing the Line: Direct Estimation of Cross-Border Cigarette Sales and the
Effect on Tax Revenues.** **Access: full July 2008 author manuscript; publication
listing/abstract.** The [author-hosted PDF](https://www.oxy.edu/sites/default/files/assets/Economics/Chiou/chiou_and_muehlegger_crossborder.pdf)
and [author-deposited record](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1312404)
identify the *B.E. Journal of Economic Analysis & Policy* contribution, volume
8, article 48 (2008).

This is a particularly important precedent for **income-sensitive destination
choice**. The location model interacts price and distance with income and uses
observed purchase locations and consumer demographics (PDF pp. 17-18).
PDF p. 20 reports that most income interactions are insignificant in the
alternative specifications discussed there. Do not turn inclusion of
heterogeneity into evidence of a robust income gradient.

**Overlap:** direct purchase-jurisdiction choice with income heterogeneity under
tax-price differences, not just traffic. **Boundary:** cigarette excises and a
structural location model, not a general local-sales-tax event study. The
verified outcomes do not measure venue income composition or market-wide
mixing.

### 6. Iksoo Cho, NTA proceedings/2014 draft

**Local Sales Tax, Cross-Border Shopping, and Distance.** **Access: full
[NTA-hosted draft](https://ntanet.org/wp-content/uploads/proceedings/2014/047-cho-local-sales-tax-cross-shopping.pdf);
cover undated, 2014 proceedings path.** PDF pp. 7-9 describe Nebraska city
taxable-sales data; p. 12 defines city gross taxable sales and sales per filing
as outcomes. Personal income is an area-level control, not a customer-income
composition outcome. PDF pp. 4-5 describe geographic differences, including
failure to reject the stated null for remote cities.

**Overlap:** local rather than solely state taxation, alternative shopping
locations, and distance-dependent substitution. **Boundary:** shopping is
inferred from aggregate sales, not observed income-specific person-to-venue
flows. No customer-composition or mixing outcome is verified. This is a
conference draft, not a verified peer-reviewed publication; its inferred
cross-border effects should not be relabelled direct mobility evidence.

### 7. Esteban Moro, Dan Calacci, Xiaowen Dong and Alex Pentland (2021)

**Mobility patterns are associated with experienced income segregation in large
US cities**, *Nature Communications* 12, 4633, **30 July 2021**,
DOI 10.1038/s41467-021-24899-8. **Access: full
[publisher PDF](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-24899-8.pdf)**;
[article record](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-24899-8).

PDF pp. 2 and 8 define place composition using time spent by visitors grouped
by **home-CBG median household income**, and individual experienced segregation
using visited places. The paper relates mobility exploration to income
segregation. PDF p. 8 explicitly calls the results descriptive rather than
causal and distinguishes potential encounters from different kinds of actual
interaction.

**Overlap:** income-proxy destination composition and experienced mixing are
established measurement topics. **Boundary:** no sales-tax treatment is verified;
this does not answer the tax question. Its time-weighted measures are not
automatically interchangeable with annual visit-weighted origin shares or a
market-wide within/between-venue accounting measure.

## Remaining verification and safe positioning

1. **Resolve Miller-Omartian before contribution positioning.** Obtain a public
   or expressly shareable manuscript/appendix and check origin-income links,
   heterogeneous mobility, venue clientele and market-level outcomes. Neither
   the title nor its absence from this search answers those questions.
2. **Resolve version gaps.** The final main texts of Baker-Johnson-Kueng and
   Agarwal-Marwell-McGranahan, plus the Singapore full text, remain to be
   checked for additional composition analyses. Earlier versions are labelled,
   not silently substituted for published articles.
3. **Keep the proposed increment precise.** A candidate contribution would
   concern whether tax-driven reallocations change the distribution of income
   origins across destinations and the market's potential exposure, not the
   already-studied existence of shopping responses or income heterogeneity.
   This remains a hypothesis. Income proxies are not personal income; potential
   exposure is not contact, friendship or attitudes. No welfare or causal
   mixing conclusion follows from this literature check.

Search scope combined the named titles/authors with sales tax, mobility,
SafeGraph, foot traffic, clientele, income segregation and cross-border
shopping, retaining only primary documents above. It is not a systematic
review. Full-text access means a document was retrieved, **not** read
cover-to-cover: review targeted relevant definitions, findings and limitations.
Retrieved PDFs had page-preserving text checks; key listing, heterogeneity,
cross-border and mixing-method pages were visually inspected. No raw provider
data or replication datasets were opened, and no other project file was edited.
