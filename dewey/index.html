Recommended direction

Develop one descriptive paper about where mixing occurs and who experiences it. The most promising organising question is: does a neighbourhood that attracts a diverse crowd also give its own residents diverse experiences when they go out? Establish the venue-level facts first, then compare the destination and resident perspectives on consistent geography and coverage. Access and venue variety would be possible explanations within this paper, not separate searches for significant results.

The original question remains important: do public-facing activities expose people to different social groups, and does that relate to attitudes? The current data are stronger on the composition of visits than on actual worker-customer interaction or changes in attitudes. The recommendation is therefore to make the descriptive exposure question the main contribution, while retaining the workforce and attitude analyses as qualified secondary evidence. This is a proposed direction for coauthor discussion, not an agreed change of paper or a claim that novelty has been established.

First, independently validate the remaining upstream data construction. Several audits and a substantial geography correction have already been completed. The outstanding check is the construction of annual origin flows and inherited weights from the original weekly files. Literature and design work can proceed alongside this audit; new substantive claims should wait for the relevant checks.

Ranked research ideas

Ranks reflect likely contribution, proximity to usable data and implementation risk, not expected statistical significance. Ideas 1-4 are components of one possible paper; ideas 5-8 are alternatives or later extensions. Each title links to the detailed assessment below.

Priority zero: establish confidence in the data

What is already established

The 2017-19 files are not unaudited. Initial checks examined schemas, missingness and distributions. A major subsequent correction addressed a mismatch between 2020 mobility-origin CBG identifiers and older ACS geography. The direct identifier join had omitted roughly one-third of recorded origin-visit weight from demographic matching. A separate NHGIS-based reconstruction rebuilt demographic indices for all 9,537,963 POI-years, retaining the originals and recording 109 checks. The October final-file audit then examined keys, support, valid indices, weights and within/between accounting. These are saved completed checks, not tests rerun for this plan.

However, the corrected index builder starts from existing annual origin-flow maps and carries forward fields including weeks observed and annualised matched visits. The remaining assurance gap lies upstream:

Original weekly mobility records
    -> annual origin-to-venue flows and coverage weights [independent replication needed]
    -> geography-linked demographic indices              [rebuilt and checked]
    -> resident/destination summaries and models         [audited in specific exercises]

The project record attributes the original construction pipeline to Tommy. That does not establish the exact code version and parameters of every production run. The Hamilton regression benchmark used processed outputs; it was not an independent raw-data reconstruction. Production code, configuration, source releases and execution receipts need to be pinned before assigning more precise responsibility.

The replication exercise

  1. Freeze the production lineage. Identify the exact weekly releases, scripts, parameters and annual outputs. Check duplicates, visits versus visitors, week coverage, suppression, category changes and the minimum-weeks setting. A setting in an instruction is not proof it was used.
  2. Choose a validation sample in advance. Cover all three years, venue types, geographies, coverage levels and changed census boundaries. For each selected venue-year, include all weeks required by the production definition. One weekly file cannot validate annual aggregation. Confirm exact restricted-input scope before processing; originals remain read-only.
  3. Write an independent reference calculation. Reconstruct annual flows from the written rules, then apply the corrected demographic mapping and formulas. Compare the annual-flow stage first and corrected indices second. Expected differences from the geography correction are not replication failures.
  4. Explain material discrepancies. Predeclare exact comparisons, numerical tolerances and what would require a larger sample or rebuild. Keep identifiable row-level diagnostics private. Release reviewed aggregate mismatch counts, weight discrepancies, coverage and diagnoses.
  5. Assess usability. Examine distributions, extreme weights, missing origins, continuity and common-support sensitivity. Missing venue IDs do not prove closure; appearance does not prove opening. Passing a sample audit does not certify every record or provider representativeness.

Deliverable: one audit matrix with stage, source/version, independent check, result, unresolved issue and implications for each analysis. Reproducing a hash or rerunning identical code is not an independent correctness test.

Two limitations remain consequential. The 933 values labelled MSA_CODE include metropolitan, micropolitan and unmatched areas, with assignment/vintage unresolved. Also, final exports retain the shares needed for the four-group racial decomposition, but not the ten income-bin shares needed for the equivalent income decomposition. Neither gap can be filled by relabelling a column or inferring shares from a scalar index. Audit evidence

Detailed assessment

1. Residential geography and everyday mixing

Question and economic interest. Which places bring together visitors from different home areas, beyond the opportunities created by where residents live? A diverse city may contain diverse venues because its population is diverse, or offer many opportunities to sort into different venues. Separating these descriptions is more informative than another national venue ranking.

What exists. Category comparisons, resident-weighted exposure, corrected geography and a within/between-venue decomposition. On audited common support, visit-weighted racial fractionalization within venues is 0.4358, versus 0.4811 after pooling visitors within area-subcategory-year cells. The between-venue component is 9.41% of the pooled value. This is accounting, not an effect on attitudes. Existing findings

What would be new here. A consistent comparison of venue mixing, resident exposure and an independently constructed residential opportunity benchmark. Comparing experienced and residential segregation is not itself new: Athey et al. (2021) establish their relationship, and Massenkoff and Wilmers (2025) study class encounters, firms and distance. Our precise distinction must survive a methods-level comparison.

Implementation. Fix groups and denominators; show annual and common-support distributions, complete subcategory results and a top-category summary. Build a residential benchmark from independently measured population composition and a declared accessibility/allocation rule. Compare observed and benchmarked exposure on identical support, showing raw levels as well as differences. Dividing venue indices by an MSA/subcategory mean is not equivalent to controlling for residential opportunity and can remove the category variation of interest.

Feasibility and decision. Descriptive replication is close to existing data; a credible opportunity benchmark requires menu and catchment construction. Deliver three figure families: residential versus activity-space exposure, within/between venue mixing, and venue versus resident perspectives. Continue only if a stable substantive distinction remains beyond existing papers. If it depends on one benchmark or merely reproduces a known fact, narrow the claim rather than searching for a significant category.

2. Neighbourhoods as destinations and origins

Question and economic interest. Can a neighbourhood serve a diverse set of visitors while its residents visit relatively homogeneous places? The reverse is also possible. This distinguishes the social role of a location from the activity spaces of its residents, potentially changing how we interpret amenities and segregation without requiring an attitude effect.

What exists and what is missing. Resident and destination aggregations exist; a verified matched neighbourhood comparison using the same years, geography, universe and weights does not. The increment is that comparison, not directional flows themselves: Nie et al. (2025 preprint) already analyse directional racialised mobility in New York City. Prior aggregation work

Implementation. For each neighbourhood-year, calculate (a) average diversity within its venues, weighted by covered incoming visits, and (b) average diversity of venues visited by residents, weighted by covered outgoing visits. Use the same venue index for both and display both axes before a difference score. Separately show the pooled incoming profile: diverse visitors across separate homogeneous venues need not mix within venues. Fix comparable coverage rules before inspecting results; missing or poorly observed flows are not zeros.

First outputs. A weighted scatterplot and distribution of matched differences, with residential composition and support shown; full area/category summaries rather than selected examples; separate 2017, 2018 and 2019 checks. Start with continuous measures. Any high-incoming/low-outgoing labels should use explained, predetermined thresholds. Check dependence on central business districts, tourism and very small resident populations.

Feasibility and decision. This requires approved origin-to-venue flows, not only scalar POI indices. Broader neighbourhood aggregation may be more reliable than fine cells. Continue if both sides have substantial comparable coverage and the distinction survives boundary/support sensitivity. Defer if only a heavily selected unmatched comparison is possible. Weekday/weekend composition is a later option: joint fields and entitlement must first be verified, and weekdays do not identify work trips.

3. Access to mixed venues

Question and economic interest. Do lower-income home areas have fewer reachable opportunities for mixing, or do visits differ despite similar measured opportunities? This separates constraints from use. An unexplained residual is not automatically a preference for segregation.

What we already tried. Nearby shared-grocery comparisons in 11 selected counties found lower exposure to top-fifth-income home areas for visitors from lower-income home areas. About half the gap remained within relative-distance bins. Historical menu and route checks then failed coverage requirements. Another distance-controlled regression is not a new mechanism test. Existing results and failed checks

Required data. A historical venue list independent of observed visits, reliable locations/categories and appropriately dated travel costs. Observed prices, formats, hours and capacity would improve it if available. Present-day listings are not automatically a 2017-19 menu; visited venues are not the full set of alternatives.

Implementation. Start with one category and areas selected for source coverage. Construct travel-time/distance bands and the distribution of accessible venue diversity under a declared rule. Compare observed exposure with a transparent allocation benchmark within that menu. Assess menu coverage, boundaries and crowd-measure noise. If defining a mixed venue from its crowd, check stability across time or independent composition samples where possible; do not treat the label as exogenous.

Contribution and stop rule. Distance and bridging locations feature in Massenkoff and Wilmers and Nilforoshan et al. (Nature, 2023). The increment would be the matched opportunity-versus-use distinction. Continue only with enough independently measured menu coverage under a declared rule. Retain earlier failed coverage requirements; do not loosen them after viewing effects. Preference or travel-cost parameters require identification beyond this descriptive exercise.

4. Variety within a service category

Question and economic interest. Holding the service fixed, does a larger or more differentiated venue set let visitors sort into different establishments? More options could increase diversity inside venues and separation across venues simultaneously.

Completed pilot. Within-subcategory correlations between log recorded venue count and the between-venue share of racial diversity are 0.5594, 0.5638 and 0.5676 in 2017-19. In 2019, across count bins from 2-4 to 100+ venues, the mean between fraction rises from 1.11% to 8.34%, while mean within-venue fractionalization rises from 0.2861 to 0.4162. These are different area-category cells, not entry within the same market. Pilot table

Next analysis. Distinguish establishment count, category breadth and within-category format/price variety. Resolve geography and provider coverage first. Show counts alongside population, density, geographical size and residential composition. Examine comparisons within defined areas and services, adjusting both outcomes and regressors when estimating fixed effects. Changes over three years matter only once continuity and genuine count changes can be distinguished from backfill or sampling. Observed count is not automatically the feasible choice-set size.

Novelty and structural feasibility. Nilforoshan et al. already discuss differentiated venues. Couture, Dingel, Green and Handbury (January 2026) estimate demographic preferences using individual direct home-based trips and within-chain comparisons. Our annual moments do not reproduce that identification. A model needs credible menus, travel costs, unobserved quality, prices/capacity and an approach to endogenous patron composition and supply.

Decision. Keep this as a bounded descriptive mechanism check, with within- and between-venue results side by side. Consider structural estimation only after an explicit identification argument and required variation survive the audit. A count correlation alone is insufficient.

5. Historical persistence around Green Book places

Question and economic interest. Do places historically listed as welcoming Black customers retain distinctive patronage today, even after the business disappears? This could examine persistent commercial networks and institutions. Historical access is not the same as an integrated crowd.

Current position. Literature/design assessment only: no historical-modern linkage or regression. The authors’ release, licence and usable download remain unverified. Close predecessors include Cook et al. (QJE, 2023), Jones et al. (JUE, 2024) and Competition and Consumer Discrimination (2025). A modern-persistence contribution needs a focused citation check.

Feasibility exercise. Verify lawful access and the codebook, then select a few cities by historical address coverage before viewing modern outcomes. Check sources against the NYPL guides. Separate continuously operating businesses, replacements at the same site, demolished/noncommercial sites and surrounding districts. Retain every nonmatch and disappeared site; selecting survivors would condition on historical development.

Potential analysis. Describe modern Black-origin visitation and within-venue diversity separately. Compare listed sites with justified alternatives of similar historical business type and neighbourhood context. An unlisted business is not necessarily discriminatory; a Black-serving venue is not necessarily integrated. Location selection, residential change and redevelopment confound causal interpretations.

Decision. Best separate alternative for a small linkage audit, but not currently the most feasible paper. Continue only with reproducible coverage, acceptable linkage and a distinct question. Historical results do not cleanly separate customer prejudice, owner prejudice and changing demand; listing status does not identify those mechanisms for us. Feasibility record

6. Weather, seasonality and composition

Question. When weather changes, do people make fewer trips, switch destinations or encounter different crowds at the same destination? Distinguishing margins could show when constraints alter mixing rather than only activity volume.

Novelty problem. Baldenius, Klauber and Koch, Heat and experienced racial segregation (JEEM, 2025) already study weekly mobility in 315 US metropolitan areas in 2018-20, finding increased segregation during heat, particularly for lower-income origins and leisure destinations. A generic temperature/racial-mixing regression would be very close. A volume/destination/composition decomposition or justified income-origin dimension could differ, but novelty is not established.

Data and implementation. Verify origin-by-time composition at the intended frequency. Annual indices are insufficient; daily/hourly totals plus weekly origin shares do not identify daily/hourly crowds. With suitable weekly inputs, link dated weather such as PRISM, fix geography/calendar comparisons and distinguish unexpected weather from seasonality and long-run adaptation. Specify which margins the data can identify before choosing a decomposition.

Decision. Produce a literature-overlap and schema assessment first. Proceed only with a distinct question and joint temporal composition. Otherwise use weather as context or robustness, not a new paper. No completed weather-mixing estimate is available in this project plan.

7. Urban renewal and historical transport

Question. Do historic clearance, highways or transport connections leave persistent differences in access to commercial meeting places and patronage? The channel could be spatial organisation of residents and amenities rather than attitudes.

Data and overlap. Renewing Inequality has project/displacement records and partial boundaries, with imputed annual displacement timing. USGS topoView supplies maps, not a ready-made transport instrument. Collins and Shester (2013) examine urban-renewal effects; Aiello et al. (2025) examine highways and online ties. Venue patronage could be an incremental outcome, but infrastructure effects are established territory.

Feasibility exercise. Select cities by coverage; verify boundaries/timing and potential comparison corridors or districts. Distinguish destruction of destinations from altered access and residential sorting. Report boundary uncertainty and redevelopment before specifying an event study or instrument.

Decision. High cost and identification risk make this a reserve option. Placement is endogenous; effects on income, residence and land use threaten exclusion restrictions. Earlier modern transport pilots had failed or weakened support/mechanism checks. A historical label does not repair those problems. Start only with a specific defensible comparison.

8. Ancestral exposure and attitudes

Question. Does parents’ or grandparents’ social environment relate to descendants’ beliefs about competition and redistribution? This is transmission, not exposure on a contemporary visit.

Prior work and overlap. Earlier regressions already used parents’ grow-up locations. The saved summary reports a positive luck-versus-effort association but no exposure-segregation association for the incentivised zero-sum question. It does not validate historical exposure timing. Chinoy et al. (AER, 2026) already analyse family history and historical roots of zero-sum thinking. Earlier attitude work

Required data. Verify released geography and the earlier regressors’ construction. The questionnaire asks childhood town/state, not exact ancestral neighbourhoods. Usable geography in the replication release remains unverified here. Logan-Parman data offer county context for 1880 and 1940, conditional on compatible cohorts.

Implementation and decision. First produce a cohort-place matching and sample-selection audit without outcome regressions. Separate segregation from group shares and already studied historical channels. Do not assign 2017-19 mobility exposure to an ancestor. Continue only with a new, well-timed measure and enough support; otherwise retain the earlier attempt and defer.

Measurement and empirical approach

Use comparable objects. Let D[j,t] be venue diversity and v[o,j,t] the covered flow from origin neighbourhood o to venue j in year t.

Venue diversity D[j,t] = 1 - sum of squared group shares at venue j

Resident exposure R[o,t]
  = sum over destinations of v[o,j,t] * D[j,t]
    divided by the origin's total covered destination flow

Destination exposure V[a,t]
  = sum over venues in area a of total covered flow to j * D[j,t]
    divided by total covered flow to venues in area a

R follows residents wherever they travel; V follows visits to venues in an area regardless of visitors’ residence. Neither observes actual conversation. An R - V comparison requires the same index, period, venue support and flow convention. Show both components and coverage separately.

Keep income and race separate. Income refers to home-CBG income, not personal earnings. The ten bins use unweighted CBG-income ranks, not equal-population deciles. The racial index uses four groups (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic Asian and Hispanic), conditional on retained group mass. It is not an unconditional index of the entire population. Origin-based allocation is not observed individual identity.

Weights change the question. Visit weighting describes recorded activity; equal-venue weighting describes the average recorded establishment. Neither automatically represents the average person or county. Converting origin visitor counts to visits using a venue-wide visits-per-visitor ratio assumes equal frequency across origins within that venue. Exclude missing-income origins from the relevant mean and renormalise over covered origins; report lost weight rather than imputing zero.

Figures and support first, then regressions. A possible descriptive specification is:

diversity[j,t] = subcategory effects + area-by-year effects
                + pre-specified accessible-residential composition
                + observed venue characteristics + residual[j,t]

This is proposed, not newly estimated. Area-by-year effects do not absorb neighbourhood-specific access. Identify the exact regressor source and residualise both outcomes and regressors for fixed-effects estimation. Venue-invariant category effects cannot also be identified with venue fixed effects. Inference must reflect regressor variation and clustering, not treat millions of rows as independent information.

An allocation benchmark is different from adding residential controls. Specify its menu/travel rule; do not use the same observed visitor composition as an independent benchmark for itself. For directional comparisons, assess leaving out the focal origin’s contribution and record lost support. Decompositions remain conditional accounting, not causal estimates of tastes or policy effects.

Local access attempts

In 11 selected counties across 2017-19, nearby shared-grocery comparisons found roughly 1.7-2.3 percentage-point lower exposure to top-fifth-income home areas for the lower-income-origin group. Around half remained within relative-distance bins. This is selected-sample evidence, not personal-income sorting or identified preferences. Historical menu and route coverage checks failed. Outcomes, workforce and contrary evidence

Positive conditional economic-connectedness associations exist in note 56; they are not a uniform null or a causal effect. Held-out prediction in note 64 improved by 3.856% with Atlas child-count weights and 2.262% with equal-state weights, but worsened by 2.313% with equal-county scoring. The all-three validation requirement failed. Correction 70a specifies Atlas child counts, not Facebook-user weights.

Current-county attitude exposure coefficients did not survive the recorded multiple-testing adjustment. Calendar-aligned attitude work failed the support requirement before estimating coefficients: a stopped design, not a null estimate. Earlier parental-location findings are in 05_stantcheva_regression_summary.md; that workbook was not reopened for this plan.

Expected workforce data come from industry/occupation-linked population estimates, not observed venue employees. O*NET public-facing importance is not contact frequency; required education is not observed attainment. All four public-facing concentration screens failed the predeclared 10% maximum-information-share rule (maxima 16.31%, 18.17%, 29.03% and 28.52%). No corrected public-facing coefficient was estimated. Sources: notes 73-74. More venues do not create more independently varying industry scores.

Other designs and alternatives

Sports, closures, transit, routes/facilities and strikes were explored. Traffic responses, limited support or suggestive associations did not establish a robust general composition design. Preserve these attempts rather than describe them as untried. Sources: original-objective review (note 69), transit (36), route/facility audit (42) and strikes (54).
