Dewey research project

Ancestral exposure and attitudes

19 October 2026 | Exposure, mixing and attitudes

Overview and rankingMeasures and approachData qualityEight literature reviewsSource library

Review date: 19 October 2026. Literature and design assessment only. No respondent records, historical census files or restricted inputs were opened, and no model was estimated. The recommendation is for integration, not a change to the main plan.

Question and direct overlap

Can childhood exposure to separation across groups shape beliefs that persist into the next generation, independently of the descendant’s own environment? This is narrower than asking whether ancestral location predicts attitudes. The latter relationship can arise from inherited wealth, selective migration, durable institutions or reporting patterns without transmission of beliefs through contact. Establishing the first question requires a correctly timed ancestral measure and evidence that distinguishes those pathways.

A1. Chinoy, Nunn, Sequeira and Stantcheva, AER 2026, “Zero-Sum Thinking and the Roots of US Political Differences.” This is the published title, not “Political Divides.” Selected final-text survey and historical sections were inspected. Tables 4 and 8-10 already link respondent, parent and grandparent childhood counties to historical immigration, enslavement and Southern migration/culture using individual OLS and county-clustered inference. Childhood windows differ across generations. The principal zero-sum index combines four domains; it is not interchangeable with an incentivized item. These are historical associations, not randomized ancestral exposure. Publisher record; published author copy.

A2. Logan and Parman, Journal of Economic History 2017, “The National Rise in Residential Segregation.” Publisher full-text measurement sections use neighboring households in census enumeration order, comparing opposite-race adjacency with integrated and segregated benchmarks. The 1880/1940 measure captures household spatial sorting rather than only area racial composition. It is neither direct interaction nor an all-purpose measure of schools, workplaces or public-space exposure. PDF extraction was partly garbled; the legible publisher HTML supplied the methods check. Publisher full text.

A3. Luttmer and Singhal, AEJ: Economic Policy 2011, “Culture, Context, and the Taste for Redistribution.” Inspected final-paper Sections I-II compare immigrants’ redistribution preferences with origin-country averages using European Social Survey rounds 2002-2007, residence-country effects and origin-clustered uncertainty; second-generation comparisons examine persistence. The interpretation requires absence of correlated omitted origin influences. This provides a useful origin-versus-destination framework, not random migration or identification of a particular childhood contact mechanism. Author-hosted published text.

A4. Nunn and Wantchekon, AER 2011, “The Slave Trade and the Origins of Mistrust in Africa.” Inspected published sections link Afrobarometer trust to historical ethnic-group exposure, use historical coastal distance as an instrument, and distinguish ancestry-linked exposure from exposure attached to current location. The exclusion restriction and migration assumptions are substantive; neither ethnicity nor historical geography randomly assigns present beliefs. This is a methodological predecessor, not evidence about US segregation specifically. Author-hosted published text.

A5. Zanella, Journal of Comments and Replications in Economics 2025, replication of Nunn-Wantchekon. The inspected paper reproduces original-wave OLS and 2SLS but fails to reproduce some central results in subsequent waves, matching countries, groups and available question wording. It distinguishes computational replication from persistence across measurement occasions. Later samples and question availability still differ; this is not proof that every original finding was erroneous. The PDF gives publication on 16 April; the journal webpage gives 30 April. Both identify 2025. Journal article; inspected text.

A6. Mousa, Science 2020, “Building social cohesion between Christians and Muslims through soccer in post-ISIS Iraq.” Inspected intervention and results sections compare Christian teams assigned additional Muslim versus Christian players. Cooperative-team effects on football-related behavior did not generalize uniformly to stranger-directed behavior or attitudes, although the national-unity index improved. The experiment concerns structured interaction, not passive co-presence or ancestral transmission. Published paper.

A7. Enos, PNAS 2014, “Causal effect of intergroup contact on exclusionary attitudes.” Published methods randomize repeated Spanish-speaking confederate presence across commuter train-time units, with pre/post surveys. Exclusionary responses increased in this specific short-run setting; possible attenuation over time was less precisely established. The result prevents treating any additional exposure as necessarily prosocial, but does not determine the sign of intergenerational effects. Published full text.

A8. Citation-status warning. Giuliano and Spilimbergo’s 2014 “Growing up in a Recession” was retracted. The publisher’s 2023 notice reports that findings could not be replicated, likely because of a coding error. It is excluded as affirmative evidence for formative-years belief formation. Retraction notice.

Contribution that would actually remain

A parental-county regression with a different historical covariate is not enough to distinguish this proposal from A1. The strongest remaining question is whether spatial separation conditional on group composition during a well-defined formative period predicts descendants’ beliefs in a way not accounted for by current and own-childhood environments. That would replace a broad historical-location story with a specific measurement distinction. It still would not establish contact as the transmission mechanism without additional evidence.

The own-project boundary is important. Saved historical notes already describe parental-location regressions: a raw luck-versus-effort association, no significant exposure-segregation relationship for the incentivized zero-sum outcome, and a negative parental income-residential-segregation association rather than an exposure result. These are historical reported results with unresolved timing and construction limitations, not rerun or validated findings here. Separately, saved current-county attitude associations failed Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment; a calendar-aligned design stopped for inadequate support before estimation. A support failure is not a null effect, and none of these records should be overwritten by a favorable ancestral specification.

Required data and timing

A credible linkage needs independently documented childhood location and years for each generation, questionnaire definitions, recall/missingness flags, the exact outcome and survey wave, and historical geography matched to those years. Public paper access does not establish that usable ancestral geography is released or licensed. This review did not inspect survey data or acquire historical segregation files. The project record does not establish a validated cohort-aligned ancestral exposure panel.

Attaching today’s mixing score to a parent’s childhood county measures a current attribute of an ancestral place, not the parent’s childhood experience. Attaching 1940 segregation to every parent has a similar problem when formative years were much later. Interpolation between distant historical snapshots is a model of persistence, not an observation. If the period match cannot be defended, rename the estimand as ancestral-place legacy and avoid childhood-treatment language.

Aggregation within families also matters. An average across two parents or four grandparents treats missing ancestors and mixed geographic histories in particular ways. Rules must be fixed before outcome inspection, with separate accounting for known, uncertain and foreign locations. Historical boundary harmonization should preserve uncertainty rather than allocate every reported town to a falsely precise modern county. Families with recoverable histories may differ systematically in education, migration and interest in ancestry.

Identification and mechanism tests

The observational baseline should distinguish historical segregation, historical group shares, own childhood environment and current environment, explaining whether each control is a confounder or a possible mediator. Automatically controlling for present wealth or education could block part of the hypothesized transmission; omitting them leaves an economic inheritance explanation. Estimate clearly labeled associations under alternative causal interpretations rather than announcing that a long control list isolates culture.

Movers offer variation between ancestry and current place, but migration, destination choice and family formation are selected. Within-destination comparisons reduce common contemporary influences without randomizing ancestral experience. Mother-versus-father differences cannot identify transmission channels when parental histories share geography or differ in recall quality. A genuine mechanism test would need a separate measure of parental beliefs, experienced interaction or transmission, not merely another historical exposure proxy.

A causal upgrade would require a specific, independently justified source of historical variation and documented exposure by cohort. School assignment, residential relocation or an institutional boundary might be candidates, but each must survive its own selection and exclusion audit. A historical instrument that changes wealth, schooling and migration directly is not automatically an instrument for social contact. No such design has been established for this proposal.

Outcome validity deserves equal priority. Luck-versus-effort beliefs, redistribution preferences, general zero-sum thinking and incentivized decisions are not interchangeable confirmations. Specify one primary construct, a small outcome family and a multiplicity rule. Use repeated survey waves as an out-of-sample stability check where definitions permit, not as opportunities to choose the most favorable sample. The A5 comparison makes this a concrete design requirement rather than generic robustness language.

Outcome-blind decision and recommendation

Before any separately authorized pilot, produce a variable-and-cohort crosswalk from documentation alone. A later blinded feasibility audit should assess the number of independent historical places, ancestor-year support, missingness, within-destination variation and effective precision without reading outcome associations. Fail the gate if credible temporal alignment or independent exposure variation is absent. Do not treat a large respondent count as evidence of many independent historical shocks.

The recommendation is to defer this idea as a standalone causal paper. A well-defined measurement comparison could eventually be useful, including an informative null, but it needs stronger temporal construction and a clearer contribution beyond A1. The contact experiments supply interpretive discipline, not a prediction that ancestral mixing must improve attitudes.
