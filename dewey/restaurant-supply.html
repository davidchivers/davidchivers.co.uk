The result in plain English

Larger metropolitan areas have more restaurants, but full-service restaurant numbers rise almost in proportion to the number of residents. Across 308 geographically comparable metros, doubling population is associated with about 2.03 times as many full-service establishments, or 1.6% more per resident. The model-based interval includes exact proportionality.

This does not refute the proposed choice mechanism. Twice as many venues can still allow more sorting, even if population also doubles. A large positive relationship between city population and venue counts is therefore not sufficient evidence for that mechanism. We must measure accessible alternatives and use of those alternatives, not mistake the mechanical size relationship for evidence of segregation.

Data and model

The outcome is the published count of employer establishments in each of four fixed 2017 NAICS industries in the 2019 County Business Patterns metropolitan file. The regressor is log metropolitan population, constructed from Vintage 2019 county population. There is one observation per metro, one cross-section, equal metro weights, and an intercept. There are no individual incomes, visits, worker profiles, attitude outcomes, year effects or other controls in these regressions.

For each industry separately:

log⁡(Nm)=α+βlog⁡(Pm)+εm. \log(N_m)=\alpha+\beta\log(P_m)+\varepsilon_m.

Here NmN_m is establishments and PmP_m is residents. The reference value is beta = 1, which means proportionate supply, not beta = 0. Doubling population corresponds to a fitted count ratio of 2β2^\beta, and to 100[2β−1−1]100[2^{\beta-1}-1]% more establishments per resident. These are cross-sectional associations, not effects of making a city bigger.

Sources: 2019 CBP files, CBP county-membership reference, 2017 NAICS labels, and the previously verified population construction in note 99. The prospective protocol fixed the four industries and both geographic samples before establishment counts were downloaded or read.

The four rows are the entire predeclared industry set, not selected significant examples. The primary full-service per-resident interval is approximately -0.24% to +3.41%. Limited-service supply is also close to proportional; snacks/nonalcoholic drinks have a larger positive gradient. The buffet result is conditional on publication: counts are absent for 98 of 308 otherwise eligible metros, so it is particularly vulnerable to selection by the small-cell disclosure rule. It should not be interpreted as an unconditional national buffet-supply elasticity.

The intervals describe HC3 model-based uncertainty, not Census measurement error or spatial dependence. The four estimates are descriptive comparisons, not independent confirmatory significance tests. A high R-squared largely reflects the relationship between two measures of city size.

Geographic and publication checks

CBP’s documented metropolitan reference covers 2017-2021; it is not automatically the September 2018 population geography. Of its 382 US metros, 308 have exactly matching county sets and metro status, 72 have different county membership, and two codes are absent from the 2018 reference. We do not silently match on the five-digit code. The CBP reference’s 551 micropolitan areas are excluded. The strict primary sample accounts for 74.61% of the newer reference’s metropolitan population, not all US metro residents. The broader, separately labelled sensitivity below is therefore important; boundary stability is not a random selection criterion.

For the primary sample, full- and limited-service counts cover all 308 metros. Buffets are published for 210 of the 308 eligible metros, containing 92.81% of the eligible sample’s population; snacks cover 307 metros and 99.96% of the same eligible population. Population coverage is not venue or visitor coverage. An omitted CBP cell is unknown here, not a zero. No suppressed count is reconstructed from totals.

What this changes in the research plan

Continue rank 2, but keep this as a supply benchmark. The substantive question remains whether people from different-income home areas use different reachable centres, different products, or different venues within the same centre/service. Metropolitan counts cannot distinguish these.

The next income-accounting run still requires the named annual-flow and ACS inputs, mass reconciliation and producer geography checks. A later common-menu study additionally needs independent historical centre/tenant and product information. No coefficient here is a preference parameter, a measure of co-presence, or evidence that exposure changes attitudes. Counts are establishments, not firms, seats, opening hours, or accessible menus.

This is a different use of CBP from note 25’s historical county employment regressions. Those estimates were not rerun or endorsed by this exercise. The prior racial venue-count comparison, negative equal-county validation, correction 70a and failed public-facing gates are unchanged. Home-area income is still not individual income, and expected workers are not observed staff.
